Abstract

I study the period of the decimal expansion of a rational number a/b in lowest terms — the smallest positive integer k such that the digits of a/b are eventually periodic with block length k. The starting question is: given b, how long can the period be? A short pigeonhole argument shows the period exists and is at most b; a sharper application restricted to remainders coprime to b shows that whenever b and 10 are coprime, the period is at most phi(b), where phi is Euler's totient function. The bulk of the paper develops the prime-power machinery that pins down the exact period rather than just a bound.

Key results

The first observation is that the period of a/b is governed entirely by the long-division remainder sequence r_0, r_1, r_2, ..., where r_0 = a mod b and r_(j+1) = 10 * r_j mod b. Two consecutive remainders that coincide force the digit sequence to repeat from that point on, and pigeonhole on the finite set of possible remainders is what gives existence of the period in the first place.

When b and 10 are coprime, every remainder lies in the multiplicative group of units mod b, which has exactly phi(b) elements. Pigeonhole there gives the cleaner bound: period of a/b is at most phi(b). The bound is rarely tight — for example, 1/13 has period 6 even though phi(13) = 12, and 1/21 has period 6 even though phi(21) = 12. Both are proper divisors of phi(b), which is the typical case rather than the exception.

The main theorem of the paper takes the prime factorization of b as input and returns both the period and the pre-period exactly. Write b = 2^a * 5^c * p_1^(e_1) * ... * p_r^(e_r), where the primes p_i are distinct and coprime to 10. Then the decimal expansion has exactly L = max(a, c) non-repeating digits, followed by a purely repeating block of length lcm(lambda(p_1^(e_1)), ..., lambda(p_r^(e_r))), where lambda(p^e) = lambda(p) * p^(max(e - 1 - s, 0)) and s is determined by the p-adic valuation of 10^(lambda(p)) - 1. The decomposition reduces the period question for any b to the case of a single prime power, which is then handled with a valuation-lifting lemma.

The closing sections of the paper take a group-theoretic view: the period of 1/b equals the order of 10 in the multiplicative group mod b, and many of the divisor-of-phi(b) patterns drop out cleanly from that observation. There is also a probabilistic perspective on which fraction of denominators below a given size attain various periods, which connects the elementary results to the heuristics of Artin-style conjectures for primitive roots.
