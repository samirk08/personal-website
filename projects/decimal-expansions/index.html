Abstract

I study the period of the decimal expansion of a rational number a/b in lowest terms — the smallest positive integer k such that the digits of a/b are eventually periodic with block length k. The starting question is: given b, how long can the period be? A short pigeonhole argument shows the period exists and is at most b; a sharper application restricted to remainders coprime to b shows that whenever b and 10 are coprime, the period is at most phi(b), where phi is Euler's totient function. The bulk of the paper develops the prime-power machinery that pins down the exact period rather than just a bound: a complete formula for the period and pre-period of any rational number, given only the prime factorization of its denominator, with a single "exceptional-prime" parameter s absorbing the rare cases (only p = 3 and p = 487 below 1000) where the generic formula fails.

Introduction

The starting object is the decimal expansion of a/b for integers a and b with b ≥ 2. Carrying out long division produces a sequence of remainders r_0, r_1, r_2, ..., where r_0 = a mod b and r_(j+1) = 10 * r_j mod b. The next digit of the expansion is floor(10 * r_j / b), so two equal remainders force the digit sequence to repeat from that point onward. The period is the smallest k for which the expansion is eventually periodic with block length k; the pre-period is the length of the non-repeating prefix.

Two observations from elementary number theory get the project off the ground. First, the remainders all live in {0, 1, ..., b-1}, a set of size b. Among the first b+1 terms r_0, ..., r_b, pigeonhole forces two to coincide — and once two remainders coincide the sequence is locked into a cycle from that point. So every rational has an eventually periodic decimal expansion, with period at most b. Second, when b and 10 are coprime, every r_j is itself coprime to b (induction on the recursion r_(j+1) = 10 * r_j mod b uses gcd(10, b) = 1 to preserve coprimality), so the remainders live in the group of units (Z/bZ)*, which has size phi(b). Pigeonhole there gives the cleaner bound: period at most phi(b).

The phi bound is rarely tight, and that is what motivates the rest of the paper. For b = 13, we have phi(13) = 12, but 1/13 = 0.076923 076923 ... has period 6 — exactly half the bound. For b = 21 = 3 * 7, we have phi(21) = 12, but 1/21 = 0.047619 047619 ... also has period 6. In both cases the true period is a proper divisor of phi(b), and the phi bound gives the right order of magnitude but no insight into which divisor. The project's goal is to replace that bound with an exact formula that takes the prime factorization of b as input and returns the period and pre-period of any fraction a/b in lowest terms.

Approach

The first reduction is to translate the period — a statement about decimal digits — into a statement about modular arithmetic. Multiplying a/b by 10^k shifts the decimal point k places to the right; if the expansion repeats with block length k, the fractional part of 10^k * (a/b) equals the fractional part of a/b, so (10^k - 1) * (a/b) is an integer, which is the same as saying b divides a * (10^k - 1). When gcd(a, b) = 1 we can cancel the a, and the period becomes the smallest k with 10^k ≡ 1 (mod b). That smallest k is exactly the multiplicative order of 10 modulo b, written ord_b(10), and the dependence on a drops out entirely. So 2/7, 3/7, and 6/7 all have period 6 because they all reduce to ord_7(10) = 6.

The second reduction handles factors of 2 and 5 in b. Write b = 2^alpha * 5^beta * m, where gcd(m, 10) = 1, and set L = max(alpha, beta). The pre-period is exactly L: multiplying a/b by 10^L clears all factors of 2 and 5 from the denominator, leaving a fraction with denominator m coprime to 10, which is purely periodic. The lower bound L comes from a parity argument — 10^k - 1 is always coprime to 10, so the only way b can divide 10^s * (10^k - 1) is if 10^s already absorbs the factors of 2^alpha and 5^beta in b, forcing s ≥ L. The periodic part has length ord_m(10).

The third reduction uses the Chinese Remainder Theorem to break m into its prime-power factors. If m = p_1^(e_1) * ... * p_r^(e_r) is a factorization into distinct prime powers each coprime to 10, then ord_m(10) = lcm(ord_(p_1^(e_1))(10), ..., ord_(p_r^(e_r))(10)). The proof goes both directions: any common period modulo each p_i^(e_i) lifts to a period modulo m via CRT, and conversely a period modulo m reduces to a period modulo each p_i^(e_i). So computing the period of any rational reduces to computing ord_(p^e)(10) for each prime power in the factorization of the denominator.

The hardest piece — and the structural heart of the paper — is computing ord_(p^n)(10) for a single prime power. Set lambda = ord_p(10), the order of 10 modulo p. The claim is that ord_(p^n)(10) = lambda * p^(max(n - 1 - s, 0)), where s = v_p(10^lambda - 1) - 1 and v_p is the p-adic valuation (the largest exponent k with p^k dividing the integer in question). The proof has three stages. Stage one shows ord_(p^n)(10) must take the form lambda * p^e for some 0 ≤ e ≤ n - 1, by using Fermat's Little Theorem in one direction and a binomial expansion in the other. Stage two — the valuation-lifting lemma — shows that for an integer a ≡ 1 (mod p) with v_p(a - 1) = r ≥ 1, raising a to the p-th power increases the p-adic valuation by exactly one: v_p(a^p - 1) = r + 1. Stage three iterates the lifting lemma to get v_p(10^(lambda * p^j) - 1) = 1 + s + j, then uses minimality of the order to pin down e = max(n - 1 - s, 0) exactly.

Main theorem

Putting the three reductions together gives the main result. Let a/b be a fraction in lowest terms, and write b = 2^alpha * 5^beta * p_1^(e_1) * ... * p_r^(e_r), where the p_i are distinct primes coprime to 10. Then the decimal expansion of a/b has exactly L = max(alpha, beta) non-repeating digits, followed by a purely repeating block of length lcm(lambda(p_1^(e_1)), ..., lambda(p_r^(e_r))), where lambda(p^e) = lambda(p) * p^(max(e - 1 - s, 0)) with s = v_p(10^(lambda(p)) - 1) - 1. That is the formula. It takes the prime factorization of b as input and returns the period and pre-period exactly.

A few quick examples make the formula concrete. For b = 56 = 2^3 * 7, we have alpha = 3, beta = 0, m = 7, and L = 3. The formula predicts three non-repeating digits followed by a block of length ord_7(10) = 6. Indeed, 1/56 = 0.017 857142 857142 ... with three non-repeating digits and period 6. For b = 42 = 2 * 3 * 7, we have alpha = 1, beta = 0, m = 21, L = 1, and ord_(21)(10) = lcm(ord_3(10), ord_7(10)) = lcm(1, 6) = 6. So 1/42 should have one non-repeating digit followed by a period-6 block, and indeed 1/42 = 0.0 238095 238095 ...

The most interesting structural fact in the prime-power case is the role of s. For p = 7, lambda = 6, and v_7(10^6 - 1) = v_7(999999) = 1, so s = 0. The formula reduces to the "generic" form lambda(7^n) = 6 * 7^(n-1), and you can check lambda(7) = 6, lambda(49) = 42, lambda(343) = 294. For p = 3, however, lambda = 1 (since 10 ≡ 1 (mod 3)) and v_3(10 - 1) = v_3(9) = 2, so s = 1. The formula gives lambda(3^n) = 3^(max(n - 2, 0)), which means the period stays at 1 for both n = 1 and n = 2 before starting to grow: lambda(3) = 1, lambda(9) = 1, lambda(27) = 3, lambda(81) = 9, lambda(243) = 27. A computer search over all primes below 1000 with p not dividing 10 found exactly two cases with s ≥ 1: p = 3 and p = 487. Every other prime in that range obeys the generic formula. Primes with s ≥ 1 are known as Wieferich-like primes with respect to base 10, and whether infinitely many exist is conjectured but unproven; the formula handles both the generic and the exceptional cases uniformly, with s absorbing the anomaly.

From a group-theoretic angle the same result reads cleanly: when gcd(b, 10) = 1, the period of 1/b equals the order of 10 in the multiplicative group (Z/bZ)*. Many divisor-of-phi(b) patterns drop out of that observation. The set of fractions 1/b whose period equals a fixed k corresponds to the set of b for which 10 has order exactly k in (Z/bZ)*, which is computable in terms of the cyclotomic polynomial Phi_k and connects directly to the heuristic distribution of multiplicative orders studied in algebraic number theory.

Discussion

The formula above is, as far as I can tell, the cleanest closed form for the period and pre-period that uses only the prime factorization of b and a single per-prime exceptional parameter. The structural surprise — at least the one I had not anticipated when I started working on the problem — is that exceptional primes are genuinely rare and that a single parameter s is enough to absorb them. I had expected the exceptional case to require a separate formula or a list of cases. Instead one extra valuation, computed once per prime, slots cleanly into the generic expression.

There is a probabilistic perspective worth noting. For a random prime p (chosen uniformly from primes up to some large N), the period of 1/p is distributed across the divisors of phi(p) = p - 1 in a non-uniform but predictable way. The fraction of primes for which 10 is a primitive root modulo p — equivalently, the period of 1/p equals p - 1 — is conjectured by Artin's primitive root conjecture to be a specific positive constant (the Artin constant times a correction factor for base 10). The conjecture is unproven without GRH, but the heuristic distribution it predicts matches numerical experiments well, and the period-distribution-over-primes question becomes a concrete instance of Artin's much broader conjecture.

If I were to extend the project, the natural directions are two. First, the generalization to base g rather than base 10: every result above goes through with 10 replaced by g and the splitting 2^alpha * 5^beta replaced by the prime-power factorization of g in the denominator. The exceptional-prime parameter s still controls the prime-power case, and "Wieferich-like prime with respect to base g" replaces the base-10 version. Second, an honest probabilistic model of the period as a function of b — averaging the formula over the Erdős-Kac distribution of omega(b) (the number of distinct prime factors) — gives an explicit-but-complicated expression for the expected period as b ranges over an interval. Both extensions are clean but mostly bookkeeping; the core structural content lives in the main theorem, in the lifting lemma, and in the role of s as the single parameter that distinguishes generic from exceptional behavior.
