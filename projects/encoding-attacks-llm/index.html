Abstract

Large language models are powerful tools, but their vulnerabilities to adversarial attacks present significant risks to safety and ethical AI deployment. Our research investigates encoding-based red-teaming techniques, leveraging custom ciphers to jailbreak LLMs and bypass safety protocols. By combining in-context learning and dynamic encoding schemes, we successfully tested attacks on models like GPT-4 and Claude 3.5, using the HarmBench dataset for evaluation. Our results indicate high attack success particularly for larger models with advanced reasoning abilities, highlighting critical vulnerabilities in the current safety mechanisms. We propose a layered defense strategy with static filters, dynamic detection systems, and real-time monitoring to mitigate these threats. Our findings illustrate the importance of incorporating encoding-based attacks in safety training pipelines to ensure robust and ethical AI deployment.

My role: Co-authored class project (3-person team). Designed and tested in-context cipher prompts on Claude 3.5 Sonnet using HarmBench — purely prompt-level attacks, no model modification.

Key results

The team's central finding is that the attack success rate (ASR) is sharply controlled by the number of "quiet" fixed-point terms left untouched in the cipher prompt. With only five quiet terms the ciphered prompts succeeded on roughly 80% of harmful queries in categories such as Drug Production, and around 70% in Cyber Attacks, against Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Pushing the quiet-term count up to 15 cuts those rates roughly in half — Drug Production drops to 40%, Cyber Attacks to 55% — and at 25 quiet terms the ASR collapses essentially to zero. The pattern is consistent: more recognizable structure in the cipher gives the safety classifier more surface to work with.

The team kept the architecture deliberately static: the attack does not adapt to intermediate model output, which makes ASR a clean measurement rather than a moving target across runs. We red-teamed Claude 3.5 Sonnet specifically because it represents the upper end of commercially deployed safety hardening; the same harness can be pointed at smaller open-weight models given enough compute and API credits. Across all categories — cyber attacks, chemical-weapon synthesis instructions, drug production, and other HarmBench-curated harm types — the trend with quiet-term count holds.

The defensive recommendation that comes out of the work is straightforward: encoding-style adversarial prompts should be folded into safety-training pipelines explicitly, and runtime defenses should be layered rather than monolithic. A single static input filter is brittle against in-context cipher schemes; a static filter combined with a dynamic detection system and an output-side classifier is much harder to slip through.
