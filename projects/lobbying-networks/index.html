Targeted Lobbying on Council Networks

class project MIT 14.18

Co-authored class project · 2-person team · MIT 14.18

Game Theory Network Centrality Katz-Bonacich

Abstract

A lobbyist with a limited budget must decide whom to push. Network ties mean that pressure on one council member can spill over to others. We study this problem with a static linear–quadratic network game and add a voting rule that turns members' actions into passage or failure. The main result is simple. With a linear passage rule, the cheapest plan puts the whole budget on the member with the highest Katz–Bonacich centrality with decay $\beta/c$. With any smooth strictly increasing passage rule, the small-budget target is the member with the largest slope-weighted Bonacich return $$w_j = \sum_i f'(a_i^0) M_{ij}, \qquad a^0 = M b,$$ where $a^0$ is the no-lobbying equilibrium. If $f$ is linear or baseline actions are zero, this is just Katz–Bonacich centrality. If baselines differ and $f$ is concave, the ranking can flip: a peripheral member on the fence can be cheaper to target than a central member whose action is already saturated. We show this on a three-member path and then sketch a probabilistic-vote version where the same force appears as a swing-voter slope.

My role: Co-authored class project (2-person team). Applied the existing Katz-Bonacich centrality framework to a council-network voting setting; under linear voting the optimal lobbying target is the Katz-Bonacich-maximizing node with decay beta-over-c.

Key results

The setting is a one-shot game between a lobbyist and an $n$-member council connected by an undirected weighted network $G$. Each member $i$ chooses a continuous level of support $a_i \in \mathbb{R}$ — floor speeches, whip activity, committee work — and faces strategic complementarities: the marginal benefit of supporting the bill rises when network neighbors also support it. The lobbyist publicly commits to a non-negative targeting vector $t = (t_1, \ldots, t_n)$ that shifts members' marginal benefits and pays cost $\sum_i t_i$. The institution then aggregates equilibrium actions $a^*$ into passage or failure; we want the cheapest plan that clears the threshold.

Solving the equilibrium gives $a^* = M(b + t)$ with $M = (cI - \beta G)^{-1}$, where $c$ is the own-action cost coefficient, $\beta$ is the network spillover, and $b$ encodes baseline incentives. Under a linear passage rule $\sum_i a_i \geq \bar T$, the lobbyist's problem reduces to a linear program whose corner solution puts the full budget on the member maximizing $v_i = (M\mathbf{1})_i$. Spelled out, this is the Katz–Bonacich centrality $$v = M\mathbf{1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{\beta}{c}\right)^{k} \frac{1}{c^{1}} G^{k} \mathbf{1},$$ with decay parameter $\beta/c$. Smaller $\beta/c$ weights immediate neighbors; larger $\beta/c$ pulls weight outward into deeper-network nodes. The minimum cost to clear $\bar T$ is then $C^*(\bar T) = (\bar T - v^\top b) / \max_i v_i$.

The smooth-aggregator extension is what gives the slope-weighted result. Replacing the linear rule with $\sum_i f(a_i^*) \geq K$ for any $C^2$ increasing $f$ and linearizing around the no-lobbying equilibrium $a^0 = Mb$, the LP perturbation yields a passage constraint $w^\top t \geq \Delta$ where $w_j = \sum_i f'(a_i^0) M_{ij}$ and $\Delta = K - F(a^0)$. The corner solution targets $\arg\max_j w_j$. Two natural cases collapse this back to plain Katz–Bonacich: (i) $f$ is linear, so $f' \equiv 1$ and $w = M\mathbf{1}$; (ii) $b = 0$, so all members share the same slope $f'(0)$. Outside these, slope and centrality can disagree: a concave $f$ paired with asymmetric baselines makes a peripheral fence-sitter cheaper to flip than a central but already-saturated member.

We close with a probabilistic-vote companion model where actions live in $(0,1)$ and best responses are sigmoidal. The marginal return from targeting member $i$ factors into a network term times a swing-voter term $\sigma'(a_i^*) = a_i^*(1 - a_i^*)$ — the same slope-weighting force evaluated at the post-lobbying equilibrium. A small numerical example with asymmetric beliefs shows the swing term flipping the centrality ranking: a peripheral leaf on the fence beats a central member who is already opposed. Figures (network diagrams, equilibrium plots) are illustrated in the source paper but not reproduced here.

Key relations

For reference, the central quantities are:

$$M = (cI - \beta G)^{-1}, \qquad a^0 = Mb, \qquad a^* = M(b + t).$$

$$w_j = \sum_{i} f'(a_i^0)\, M_{ij}, \qquad \arg\max_j w_j \in \arg\min_t \sum_i t_i \text{ s.t. passage.}$$
