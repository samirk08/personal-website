Abstract

Early prediction of sepsis is critical for reducing mortality but remains difficult due to the heterogeneous and sparse nature of clinical time-series data. Using the 2019 PhysioNet Challenge framework — which evaluates models using a utility function emphasizing timely alerts — we compare three approaches for early sepsis detection against a logistic regression baseline: an autoencoder with k-nearest neighbors, an LSTM, and a Transformer. The challenge's preprocessing pipeline includes forward-fill and population-mean imputation, missingness indicators, and optional temporal feature augmentations. Using a dataset of 11,000 patients, models with strong temporal inductive biases substantially outperform static or representation-learning methods. The Transformer achieved the highest normalized utility (0.578) with strong early-warning capability but a high false-positive rate, followed by the LSTM (0.261), while the autoencoder + k-NN model performed poorly (0.04). These results underscore the importance of modeling long-range physiological trajectories and highlight attention-based architectures as particularly effective for clinically meaningful early sepsis prediction.

My role: Group project (6-person team). My contribution: built the LSTM model (utility 0.261). The Transformer (utility 0.578) and Autoencoder+kNN (utility 0.04) were built by other team members.

Key results

The team evaluated three deep approaches against a logistic-regression baseline on the 2019 PhysioNet Challenge dataset of 11,000 patients (5,000 train / 1,000 validation / 5,000 test), with hourly measurements over 40 clinical features (vital signs, blood pressure, oxygenation, lab values). The challenge metric is a normalized utility function $U$ that rewards alerts in the optimal 6-hour window before sepsis onset and penalizes both late detections and false positives, summed across timesteps and normalized to $[0, 1]$ via best-possible / no-alert anchors. The headline result: the three temporal architectures span a wide utility range. The Transformer reached normalized utility $0.578$ — the strongest score, with high recall but a non-trivial false-positive rate. The LSTM reached $0.261$, demonstrating that bidirectional recurrence captures most of the per-patient temporal signal but lacks the long-range cross-patient pattern matching attention provides. The Autoencoder + k-NN baseline came in at $0.04$, only slightly above the no-alert floor, confirming that representation-learning without inductive bias toward temporal structure under-performs on this task.

The preprocessing step is shared across architectures. The dataset's central difficulty is missingness: an average of 70% of values are missing per patient, with some features (e.g. EtCO$_2$) missing in 100% of cases. We applied forward-fill imputation per feature per patient, falling back to a training-set timestep-specific population mean when no prior observation existed; we then concatenated 40-dimensional binary missingness indicators to give an 80-dimensional input vector at each hour. The class imbalance is severe — only 8.28% of training patients ever develop sepsis — so the utility metric, not raw accuracy, is the right thing to optimize.

The utility function used to evaluate every model rewards meaningfully-timed alerts and penalizes harmful or unnecessary alarm behavior. For sepsis-positive patients, utility ramps from zero to full reward when alerts occur 12 to 6 hours before onset, peaks in the 6-to-0-hour window, and decays into a penalty between 6 hours before onset and 3 hours after. For non-sepsis patients, false positives accumulate small per-timestep penalties (alarm fatigue) while true negatives are neutral. Letting $\hat{t}_p$ denote the model's predicted alert time and $t_s$ the true sepsis onset, the per-step utility is the piecewise function

$$U(\hat{t}_p,\, t_s) \;=\; \begin{cases} \tfrac{\hat{t}_p - (t_s - 12)}{6} & \text{if } t_s - 12 \le \hat{t}_p \le t_s - 6 \\[4pt] 1 & \text{if } t_s - 6 \le \hat{t}_p \le t_s \\[4pt] 1 - \tfrac{\hat{t}_p - t_s}{3} & \text{if } t_s \le \hat{t}_p \le t_s + 3 \\[4pt] -0.05 & \text{(false positive on a non-sepsis patient)} \\[4pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Per-patient utility sums across timesteps; aggregate utility normalizes against a perfect-model upper bound and a no-alert lower bound. The Transformer-based approach uses time-lagged Independent Component Analysis on the 80-dimensional features, encoder-only attention layers from the standard architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) for per-step sepsis-probability output, and a tuned decision threshold. Its $0.578$ utility reflects high early-detection sensitivity at the cost of a higher per-timestep false-positive rate. The LSTM model — Samir's contribution to the project — uses bidirectional recurrent layers feeding a sigmoid output head, trained with binary cross-entropy on the per-step sepsis-onset label, with no dimensionality reduction. Threshold tuning lifts utility from a near-zero starting point to $0.261$, peaking around threshold $0.5$.

The Autoencoder + k-NN approach uses a 160-dimensional feature space augmented with exponential-moving-averages and inter-step deltas, trains an unsupervised autoencoder to learn a low-dimensional embedding, and runs k-nearest-neighbor lookups against the labeled training set at inference time. It under-performs ($0.04$) because the embedding does not preserve the per-patient temporal structure that the utility metric explicitly rewards.

The team's takeaway is unsurprising in hindsight but worth stating: temporal inductive bias matters more than parameter count or representation cleverness for this task. Attention-based architectures dominate the leaderboard and, by separately tuning recall (via threshold) and precision (via post-processing), can be pushed to clinically meaningful early-warning lead times of 6–12 hours, the window where the literature shows antibiotic timing actually moves mortality.
