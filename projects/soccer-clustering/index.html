Abstract

I investigate whether identifiable clusters of soccer team playing styles are significantly associated with match outcomes such as win rate, goal difference, and expected-goals difference. The analysis spans the FIFA Men's World Cup 2022, UEFA Euro 2024, CONMEBOL Copa America 2024, and FC Barcelona's La Liga seasons from 2006/07 through 2020/21, with all event data scraped minute-by-minute from the public StatsBomb Python package. After extracting per-match team features (pressure, interceptions, tackles, passes, carries, goalkeeper line, possession share, expected goals, and a passing-network feature I construct from the same data), I run PCA + K-means and recover three clusters in every case study: a defensive/reactive cluster, a balanced/possession cluster, and a proactive/attacking cluster. Across all four cases I find a consistent relationship between cluster membership and outcomes — proactive teams win more and run higher goal differentials — and the differences are statistically significant via ANOVA (p-values 0.001 to 0.011) and significant via chi-square in two of the four cases. The Barcelona-across-seasons subcase is the most striking: the Guardiola-style high-press seasons cluster together with a +2.10 average goal difference and a 75.5% win rate across 204 matches, while earlier and post-peak seasons sit on lower-magnitude clusters even when they win at similar rates.

Introduction

This project started from a real moment of frustration as a fan: F.C. Barcelona, a possession-heavy heavy favorite, lost a Champions League knockout to Inter Milan's counterattacking and defensive game plan. The popular soccer-analytics narrative is that possession and high pressing dominate the modern era. The Inter result said something more nuanced — that style alone is not enough, and that a "weaker" team executing a different tactical profile can systematically beat a "stronger" team committed to the prestige style. I wanted to know whether that anecdote is just a knockout-tournament fluke or whether it generalizes: do certain playing-style clusters actually associate with better match outcomes once you control for the question across many tournaments and many teams?

Prior soccer-analytics work supplied a useful starting frame. Gollan et al. (2018) characterized English Premier League game styles into clusters and noted that top teams more often fell into possession-dominant clusters. He, Zhang, and Mao (2023) introduced the constructs of playing-style flexibility (range of styles a team can play) and imposition (sticking to one style regardless of opponent), and found that flexibility correlates with higher win rates. What both lines of work suggest is that style is real, measurable, and predictive — but neither tested whether the style-outcome link survives moving from a single multi-season league to discrete tournaments where teams play few games and stylistic identity is sharp.

The contribution of this project is empirical rather than methodological: I take a fairly standard pipeline — minute-level event aggregation, PCA, K-means, ANOVA, chi-square, bootstrap CIs — and run it across four substantively different settings (three international knockout tournaments plus one elite club across fifteen seasons) so the style-outcome question can be tested under several different sample regimes at once. The four case studies are chosen for breadth: men's World Cup 2022 (32 teams, 64 matches), UEFA Euro 2024 (24 teams, 51 matches), Copa America 2024 (16 teams, 32 matches), and FC Barcelona's La Liga seasons 2006/07–2020/21 (451 matches across 15 distinct seasons).

Approach

For each game in each competition I scrape every minute from 0 to 90, producing per-team rows that capture defensive metrics (pressure, interceptions, tackles, clearances, blocks, counterpress), passing and progression metrics (total passes, pass length, progressive carry rate), possession and tempo metrics (goalkeeper x-position, team depth, team width, possession share, entries-final-third), and shooting metrics (xG momentum, cumulative xG, shot rate, on-target rate). I aggregate to per-match per-team feature vectors and standardize. The full standardized space is the input to clustering — I do not project to PCA before clustering, because that throws away high-frequency variance the cluster boundary may depend on; PCA is used only for visualization.

One feature deserves a callout because it is custom rather than off-the-shelf. From the StatsBomb event data I construct a per-match passing network for each team, with players as nodes and edge weights equal to the number of completed passes between that pair of players. From this network I derive average clustering coefficient, average shortest path length, and average degree centrality, plus a width/depth/area summary of where the network sits on the pitch. These graph features capture how centralized or distributed a team's ball movement is, which raw possession share misses entirely — two teams can have the same possession share but completely different network topologies.

I run K-means with K from 2 to 10 and pick K by the elbow plot of within-cluster sum of squared distances; I cross-check K with a t-SNE projection. In every case study the elbow is clean at K = 3, which is also where the t-SNE plot shows three visually separate point groups. With clusters in hand I label each by inspecting feature centroids: Cluster 0 in every case ends up as the defensive/reactive style (lowest possession share, highest clearance counts, lowest passes per minute), Cluster 1 as a possession-oriented but less-incisive middle, and Cluster 2 as the proactive/attacking style (highest progressive passing, highest goalkeeper line, highest carry rates).

For interpretation I add a time-series step: I align event data by significant xG events and compute the correlation of every metric with the occurrence of a chance, both leading up to and at the moment of the chance. The "leading indicators" tell me what each cluster relies on to create chances — for example, the proactive cluster shows progressive-pass rate and average carry length as strong leading indicators, while the defensive cluster's leading indicators include clearances and opponent turnovers (i.e., they create chances on the back of forced errors). For statistical inference I use ANOVA on cluster-vs-goal-difference, a 10,000-permutation test on the same to handle the moderate sample sizes, a chi-square test on cluster-vs-win-frequency, and a bootstrap (1,000+ resamples) for cluster-specific 95% CIs on win rate and goal difference.

Results

The headline pattern holds in every case study: Cluster 2 (proactive/attacking) outperforms Cluster 0 (defensive/reactive) on both win rate and average goal difference, with Cluster 1 (possession-oriented) sitting in between. The magnitude of the gap and the statistical significance vary by tournament, but the direction does not.

For the World Cup 2022, ANOVA on cluster-vs-goal-difference is significant (F = 6.71, p ≈ 0.0017) and the permutation test confirms it (p ≈ 0.0014). The chi-square on cluster-vs-win-frequency is not significant (χ² = 1.49, p = 0.48) — Cluster 2 won 46.9% and Cluster 0 won 33.3%, but with the modest sample size and the high draw rate in tournament play, that gap does not clear the chi-square bar. The interpretation I take from the split is that style affects how decisively wins arrive (goal differentials) more than whether they happen (binary win/loss), at least at this sample size.

The Euros 2024 result is the cleanest of the four. Cluster 0 teams ran an 11% win rate and a –0.74 average goal difference. Cluster 1 teams ran a roughly 35% win rate. Cluster 2 teams ran a 54% win rate and a +0.69 average goal difference. ANOVA and the permutation test both came in below p < 0.01, and a logistic regression with cluster as the predictor showed Cluster 2 membership significantly raising win probability. The Copa America 2024 picture is similar: Cluster 0 at 12.5% win rate and –1.19 goal difference, Cluster 2 at 50% win rate and +0.58 goal difference, with ANOVA p = 0.011 and chi-square p = 0.048. Both test the bar comfortably.

The most interesting case is Barcelona across fifteen seasons. Here clustering is at the seasonal level (each "point" is one full La Liga campaign), and the three clusters have a clean historical reading. Cluster 0 corresponds to the early-Rijkaard / pre-Guardiola seasons (2004/05–2007/08): deeper average starting positions, fewer total passes, lower goalkeeper line. Average goal difference per league match was +1.35, win rate 69% over 78 matches. Cluster 1 corresponds to post-peak transitional seasons (2009/10, 2012/13, 2018/19–2020/21): higher carry counts, more direct play. Average goal difference +1.57, win rate 72.8% over 169 matches. Cluster 2 corresponds to the Guardiola-style high-press / positional-play seasons (2008/09, 2010/11, 2011/12, 2013/14, 2016/17): high goalkeeper line, high passes per minute, high width. Average goal difference +2.10, win rate 75.5% over 204 matches. ANOVA on season-level goal difference is significant (F = 5.72, p ≈ 0.0035; permutation p ≈ 0.0028). The chi-square on win-frequency is not significant — Barcelona wins most of the time regardless of style — but the magnitude with which they win varies materially across eras.

That last point is the most useful inference from the project. For an elite team that wins most matches anyway, "win rate" saturates and stops being informative; goal difference becomes the better proxy for stylistic dominance. The Guardiola seasons did not just keep winning; they won more emphatically, and the cluster structure picks that up cleanly even though chi-square on the binary win/loss outcome doesn't flag anything.

Discussion

The consistent direction across four substantively different settings is the result I am most confident in. Proactive, attacking style associates with better outcomes; ultra-defensive style associates with worse outcomes; possession-without-incision sits in the middle and is occasionally indistinguishable from the attacking cluster. Crucially, the time-series leading-indicator analysis showed that successful proactive clusters were not just blindly attacking — they retained structure (high goalkeeper line for organized pressing, tight progressive-passing distributions). Unsuccessful defensive clusters tended to lack offensive output beyond hopeful long balls, which is why they end up with negative goal differentials even when they nominally control fewer chances against.

A few limitations to be honest about. Cluster labels are an interpretation, not a ground truth — the same K-means run on a different feature subset (omitting passing-network metrics, for example) can permute which numerical label corresponds to which tactical archetype, even if the underlying partition is similar. Sample sizes for the international tournaments are modest (32 to 64 matches), which is exactly why I used permutation tests for the ANOVA in addition to the parametric F-test. Opponent quality is not controlled for; a defensive team facing five elite opponents and a defensive team facing five mid-table opponents end up in the same cluster with very different outcomes, and a fully causal version of this analysis would need a strength-of-schedule control. Finally, possession share is partly an effect of being ahead — winning teams hold the ball more in the final stretch — so some of the cluster-outcome correlation is mechanical rather than causal.

The natural next step is to fold opponent strength and game state into the feature set so the clusters describe pre-match tactical intent rather than realized in-match behavior. A second extension is testing whether flexibility — the He, Zhang, and Mao (2023) construct — adds predictive value beyond static cluster membership. The Inter-vs-Barcelona moment that started this project would, in that extended framework, show up as a flexibility-vs-imposition mismatch rather than a possession-vs-counterattack one, which is closer to the right causal story for a single knockout match.
