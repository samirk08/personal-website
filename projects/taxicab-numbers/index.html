Abstract

Taxicab numbers are a unique category of integers that can be represented as the sum of two cubes in multiple distinct ways. By analyzing taxicab numbers, we can illuminate many properties of cubic sums and the structure of modular cubes. Within this paper, we will analyze some of the properties of taxicab numbers, including determining the second taxicab number, proving that infinitely many taxicab numbers exist, creating a generating formula for taxicab numbers, and putting bounds on the growth rate and density of the set of taxicab numbers. In doing so we form a clearer understanding of the structure of the set of taxicab numbers.

My role: Co-authored class project (3-person team). Proved a mod-63 sieve result and identified a parametric family of non-trivial taxicab numbers.

Key results

A taxicab number is a positive integer $N$ expressible as a sum of two positive cubes in at least two distinct ways: there exist $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ with $a^3 + b^3 = N = c^3 + d^3$ and $\{a, b\} \neq \{c, d\}$. The canonical example, due to Ramanujan, is $$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 9^3 + 10^3.$$ We start by verifying that $4104 = 2^3 + 16^3 = 9^3 + 15^3$ is the second taxicab number — no integer strictly between $1729$ and $4104$ admits two cube-sum decompositions — and then turn to existence and density questions.

Trivial scaling ($N \mapsto t^3 N$) generates infinitely many cube-sum identities from any single one. The substantive question is whether infinitely many taxicab numbers exist that are pairwise non-trivially related — that is, not rescalings of each other. We answer this constructively by exhibiting a one-parameter family. For each positive integer $n$, set $$a_n = 1 + 6n^3,\qquad b_n = 1 - 9n^4 + ... \quad \text{(parametric family)} ,$$ with the property that $a_n^3 + b_n^3 = c_n^3 + d_n^3$ for explicit $c_n, d_n$ derived from algebraic identities on cubes. We then prove no two members of the family are scalar multiples of one another, so the family witnesses infinitely many non-trivially related taxicab numbers.

Density bounds follow from elementary counting: the number of taxicab numbers up to $X$ is bounded $$\Omega(X^{1/3}) \;\le\; \#\{\text{taxicab } N \le X\} \;\le\; O(X^{2/3}),$$ with a tighter conjectural lower bound $\Omega(X^{1/3 + \epsilon})$ supported by numerical fits. Restricting to pairwise non-trivially related taxicab numbers up to $X$ tightens the lower bound to $\Omega(X^{1/9})$; the upper bound stays $O(X^{2/3})$.

Section 5 turns to the modular structure of $a^3 + b^3$. Computing the achievable residues modulo 9 and modulo 7 and combining via the Chinese Remainder Theorem, we show $$|\{r \pmod{63} : r \not\equiv a^3 + b^3 \text{ for any } a, b\}| = 38,$$ so 38 of the 63 residue classes mod 63 contain no sum of two cubes — and therefore no taxicab number. We then prove this fraction $\tfrac{38}{63}$ is optimal: no larger modulus built from primes $3$ and $7$ does better, and adding any prime $p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$ leaves the fraction unchanged. Computational verification up to $p = 10{,}000$ supports the conjecture that $\tfrac{38}{63}$ is optimal across all moduli.

Together, the parametric family and the mod-63 sieve give us both an existence and an obstruction picture: infinitely many taxicab numbers exist (and we can write them down), but more than half of all residue classes modulo 63 are forbidden — so taxicab numbers are abundant but sparse, and their distribution carries strong modular structure.
